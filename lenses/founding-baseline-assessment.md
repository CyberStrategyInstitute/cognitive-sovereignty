# A founding-principles baseline with declared lenses

Recommendation: adopt an explicitly versioned U.S. founding-principles baseline, anchored in the 1776-1791 document sequence. Let optional perspectives change the questions, scope, and openly stated judgments. Keep the common baseline result visible. This fits CSF's aim of preserving a person's ability to think, dissent, choose, challenge authority, and recover control.

## What the baseline is

The Declaration is dated July 4, 1776; the Constitution was signed September 17, 1787; the Federalist essays were published in 1787-1788; the Bill of Rights was ratified in 1791. These are distinct sources, not documents all adopted in 1776. The Declaration supplies a justification grounded in rights and consent. The Constitution supplies institutions and an amendment route. The Bill of Rights supplies specific restraints. The Federalist Papers argue for ratification and explain institutional design; they are advocacy and interpretation, not a replacement constitution. [Declaration](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/declaration-transcript), [Constitution](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/constitution), [Federalist history](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-papers/full-text), [Bill of Rights](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript).

Use this as CSI's declared normative starting point. It does not prove that every country, religion, or political tradition endorses it, and agreement with CSI is not a test of cognitive health.

## Translate the sources into CSF questions

| Baseline principle | Question for an event | Existing CSF connection |
|---|---|---|
| Rights and consent | Can the person understand, accept, refuse, and revise the arrangement? | HO-005, T-CT-006; Domains 2, 5, 6 |
| Expression and conscience | Can the person question, dissent, seek other sources, and hold a different belief? | ST-006, T-CT-017; Domains 2, 4, 5 |
| Accountable, divided authority | Who decides, who benefits, and who can independently review the decision? | ST-001, T-CT-009; Domains 4, 5, 6 |
| Privacy and fair process | Can the person inspect relevant evidence, challenge a decision, and reach a responsible human? | T-CT-013, T-CT-016; Domains 3, 4, 6 |
| Correction and amendment | Can an error, score, rule, or delegated action be corrected without losing the ability to participate? | HO-002, HO-005; Domains 2, 4, 6 |

These are CSF design inferences from the sources, not claims that an eighteenth-century text directly regulates a modern AI product. Speech, conscience, privacy, and process anchors come from the [Bill of Rights](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights-transcript). Institutional division and revision are visible in the [Constitution](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript). The mapping supplements supporting information; it changes no domain, causal layer, threat ID, or CTSS weight.

## The strongest objection

The founding principles and the historical distribution of rights were not the same thing. The original constitutional text accommodated slavery and unequal political membership. Later amendments changed that order. If CSF treats the practical exclusions of the founding era as its desired standard, it could deny sovereignty to the very people it claims to protect. [Original Constitution](https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript), [Thirteenth Amendment](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/13th-amendment), [Fourteenth Amendment](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/14th-amendment).

The proposed response is explicit: apply the baseline's rights and agency questions to every affected person. Preserve historical exclusions as counterexamples and audit tests. That is a declared CSF methodological choice, not a claim about uniform founding-era practice. Keep a historical-source profile separate from a current-law profile. This paper does not settle modern constitutional doctrine or tell other communities what to believe.

## Review through different perspectives

The following are analytical reconstructions, performed here. Historical texts inform the questions; the figures did not review CSF, and these are not quotations or predictions of their modern political positions. Present-day roles are composites, not claims about named living people. Scores assess how well the design answers each reviewer's concern: 1 unresolved, 2 weak, 3 partly addressed, 4 addressed with a remaining test, 5 explicit and testable. This is a qualitative design rubric, not CTSS or empirical validation.

| Perspective | Challenge | Literal founding-era freeze | Versioned principles plus common-score contract |
|---|---|---|---|
| Madison-informed institutionalist | Can faction capture the scorer or turn protection into enforced conformity? | 3/5 | 5/5: preserve dissent, competing reviews, and auditable corrections |
| Hamilton-informed administrator | Can the system act effectively, or does every disagreement paralyze a decision? | 3/5 | 4/5: publish provisional judgments, name owners, and test timely review |
| Abigail Adams-informed inclusion critic | Whose voice is absent when sovereignty is defined? | 1/5 | 4/5: assess every affected person and audit missing stakeholders |
| Douglass-informed abolitionist critic | Do declared rights survive contact with the institutions that deny them? | 1/5 | 4/5: test actual refusal, recourse, and recovery rather than declared ideals |
| Present-day civil-liberties reviewer | Can a user reject the scorer's ideology without being scored as impaired? | 3/5 | 5/5: score mechanisms and agency effects, retain counterevidence and appeal |
| Present-day security operator | Can foreign influence be investigated without classifying dissent itself as hostile? | 2/5 | 4/5: separate behavior, attribution, evidence, national interest, and viewpoint |
| Present-day religious-conscience reviewer | Can voluntary religious commitments remain meaningful while coercion is examined? | 2/5 | 4/5: respect consent and conscience; test deception and practical exit separately |
| Present-day measurement reviewer | Can another analyst reproduce the score and identify why it changed? | 2/5 | 4/5: keep five inputs, source versions, lens ID, assumptions, and score history |

Madison's discussion of faction treats suppression of liberty as an unacceptable cure and favors managing its effects. Federalist 51 discusses institutional checks rather than dependence on virtuous officeholders alone. Hamilton's introduction argues for effective government while asking readers to consider reasons. These support the questions above; using them to design AI review is our inference. [Federalist 1 and 10](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-papers/text-1-10), [Federalist 51](https://guides.loc.gov/federalist-papers/text-51-60).

Abigail Adams's 1776 letter challenges unaccountable power over women. Douglass's 1852 address challenges the gap between national celebration and enslavement. Their inclusion is a test of the baseline's blind spots, not a claim that either endorsed the complete proposed method. [Adams letter](https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Adams/04-01-02-0241), [Library of Congress account and speech context](https://blogs.loc.gov/headlinesandheroes/2020/07/what-to-the-american-slave-is-your-4th-of-july/).

## Stress-test the lens mechanism itself

The scores below are provisional CTSS scenario assessments of possible misuse. They are not scores of nations, parties, religions, or historical figures. L, Ia, R, D, and RecD use the existing formula. All cases assume limited deployment, so L=2; R=3 means national-scale potential, not measured exposure. Impact, detection, and recovery are explicit scenario judgments.

| Failure scenario | Inputs L/Ia/R/D/RecD | CTSS | Why this matters | Required response |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A lens classifies disagreement with its doctrine as lost agency | 2/4/3/4/3 | 64 | A conformity test can hide inside a sovereignty tool | Require a mechanism and observed or inferred agency effect; allow dissent |
| Historical exclusions are treated as the desired rights boundary | 2/4/3/3/4 | 63 | The baseline could normalize unequal sovereignty | Assess every affected person; disclose historical limits and later correction |
| Optional lenses silently overwrite baseline inputs or weights | 2/3/3/4/3 | 58 | A trend could reflect a changed ideology rather than a changed event | Preserve baseline and lens results side by side with an explanation |
| A national-interest lens equates state power with personal agency | 2/4/3/4/3 | 64 | State sovereignty and individual sovereignty can conflict | Report the conflict instead of resolving it through an invisible weighting |

These scenarios define tests for the plugin design. They are excluded from the real-event baseline.

## Small plugin design

Use a data file, not executable plugin code. Each lens names its owner, version, sources, audience, normative assumptions, jurisdiction, questions, and baseline reference. The common CTSS formula stays fixed. A lens-specific assessment may change an input only by explaining a changed scope, consequence, or interpretation. Show baseline score, lens score, and each changed input together.

Do not infer a person's religion or politics to select a lens. Let people choose it. Do not score an identity group as a threat. Different traditions contain internal disagreement; a community lens needs community-authored sources and review before being represented as that tradition's position.

Start with `us-founding-v1` and reusable templates for nation-state, political, ideological, and religious perspectives. The templates ask questions; they do not claim to speak for all members. [Plugin data and demonstrations](README.md).

## Way forward

1. Review the baseline principle mapping and its universal application as the declared CSI choice. Keep modern legal compliance separate.
2. Test the same five events through the baseline and two contrasting lenses. Preserve all scores and reasons. Disagreement is an output to explain, not a failure to hide.
3. Test ideology substitution: exchange the favored party, nation, or faith while preserving coercion, deception, authority, and exit facts. Any unexplained score change requires correction.
4. Invite critics to reproduce scores and challenge inputs using the same evidence. Publish corrections and model failures with the same visibility as supporting cases.
5. Expand lens packs only after the record format, side-by-side display, and history tests pass. No new domain, causal layer, or weighting scheme is required.
